A rental property can look cheap... and still be a terrible investment.

And sometimes, a house that costs a little more can actually be the better deal.

So before you buy your first rental, there’s one question you need to be able to answer:

Is this property actually going to make money?

In this video, I’m going to show you a simple way to look at a rental deal without making the math complicated.

And at the end, we’ll break down a hypothetical one-hundred-and-twenty-thousand-dollar rental, renting for fifteen hundred dollars a month, and decide whether I’d buy it, pass on it, or negotiate.

Here’s the first thing I want you to remember.

A low purchase price does not automatically mean you’re getting a good deal.

A lot of new investors see a cheap house and immediately get excited.

But the purchase price is only the beginning.

The number that really matters is what’s left after all the bills are paid.

So let’s start with rent.

You want a realistic rent number.

Not what the seller says you might get.

Not the highest rent you can find online.

And definitely not the number you need just to make the deal look good.

Look for similar rentals in the same area.

Similar number of bedrooms and bathrooms.

Similar square footage.

Similar condition.

And, most importantly, a similar location.

Let’s say comparable homes are renting for fourteen hundred, fourteen seventy-five, and fifteen fifty.

I’d probably underwrite the property around fifteen hundred a month.

Could it rent for more? Maybe.

But I would rather have the deal work using a conservative number.

For our example, the purchase price is one hundred and twenty thousand dollars.

Expected rent is fifteen hundred dollars a month.

Now, on paper, that easily passes the one-percent rule.

One percent of one hundred and twenty thousand is twelve hundred dollars.

And our rent is fifteen hundred.

That looks pretty good.

But this is where beginners can get fooled.

The one-percent rule is a quick screening tool.

That’s it.

It tells you a property might be worth looking at.

It does not tell you how much money you’re actually going to keep.

Now we get into the expenses.

For this example, let’s estimate one hundred eighty dollars a month for property taxes.

One hundred dollars for insurance.

One hundred fifty for property management.

One hundred twenty for a maintenance reserve.

Seventy-five dollars for vacancy.

And another one hundred dollars a month set aside for bigger future repairs, like a roof, furnace, or water heater.

Add all of that up, and we’re already at seven hundred twenty-five dollars a month.

And we haven’t even paid the mortgage yet.

This is where a lot of rental-property math goes bad.

You’ll hear somebody say:

“The rent is fifteen hundred, my mortgage is six-fifty, so I’m making eight hundred fifty dollars a month.”

No.

That is not cash flow.

You still have taxes.

Insurance.

Vacancy.

Repairs.

Management.

And the big expenses that eventually show up on every property.

If you leave those numbers out, you aren’t calculating profit.

You’re just making the deal look better than it really is.

Now let’s add the financing.

For this example, we’ll assume the principal and interest payment is about six hundred thirty dollars a month.

Add that to our seven hundred twenty-five dollars in operating expenses.

Now our total estimated monthly expenses are about one thousand three hundred fifty-five dollars.

The property brings in fifteen hundred dollars.

Now look at how different the deal feels.

At the beginning, it easily passed the one-percent rule.

But after we actually ran the expenses, the cash flow is pretty thin.

Next, let’s look at cash-on-cash return.

Let’s say you put thirty thousand dollars down.

After closing costs and the money you keep in reserve, maybe you have about thirty-five thousand dollars of your own cash tied up in the deal.

If the property makes about seventeen hundred forty dollars a year in cash flow, divide that by thirty-five thousand.

That gives you roughly a five-percent annual cash-on-cash return.

Does that automatically make it a bad investment?

No.

The tenant may help pay down your mortgage.

The property could appreciate.

And rents might go up over time.

But I don’t want to buy a property because I’m hoping those things happen.

I want the deal to make sense using realistic numbers today.

And there’s one more part of the deal that a calculator can’t completely measure.

The neighborhood.

A property making three hundred dollars a month in a rough or unstable area may be a worse investment than a property making two twenty-five in a strong, stable rental area.

Look at vacancy.

Crime.

The condition of the surrounding properties.

Jobs in the area.

Schools.

Rental demand.

Property taxes.

Insurance costs.

And whether people actually want to live there.

You’re not just buying a house.

You’re buying the income that location can produce.

So... would I buy our one-hundred-and-twenty-thousand-dollar example?

At that price, with only about one hundred forty-five dollars a month in estimated cash flow, I would not rush to buy it.

I’d try to negotiate the price down.

I’d double-check that fifteen hundred dollars really is a conservative rent.

I’d verify the taxes and insurance.

And I’d make sure I didn’t miss any major repairs.

If I can lower the purchase price, improve the cash flow, and leave myself more room for surprises, then the deal becomes a lot more interesting.

And that’s really the lesson.

Don’t fall in love with the house.

Fall in love with the numbers.

Before you buy your first rental, remember:

Cheap does not automatically mean good.

Passing the one-percent rule does not automatically mean good.

And rent is not the same thing as profit.

Run the numbers conservatively.

Leave room for something to go wrong.
