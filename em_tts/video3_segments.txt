Is one hundred dollars a month in rental cash flow enough? What about three hundred? Or five hundred? There is no magic number that works for every investor, but there is a simple way to tell whether your margin is strong enough. In this video, we will compare all three.
===SCENE===
First, cash flow is what is left after rent comes in and you subtract the ongoing operating expenses and the mortgage payment. If you collect rent but ignore taxes, insurance, vacancy, maintenance, management, or debt, you are not looking at true cash flow.
===SCENE===
For this hypothetical comparison, imagine three rental properties with similar neighborhoods, condition, and risk. Deal A produces one hundred dollars per month. Deal B produces three hundred. Deal C produces five hundred.
===SCENE===
On an annual basis, that is twelve hundred dollars for Deal A, thirty-six hundred for Deal B, and six thousand dollars for Deal C. Seeing the annual number makes the difference in margin much easier to understand.
===SCENE===
A beginner may think any positive number is automatically good. But positive cash flow can still be too thin if one repair, one insurance increase, or one rent shortfall wipes out most of the year's profit.
===SCENE===
So let us stress-test all three deals with the exact same surprise: a twenty-four-hundred-dollar repair.
===SCENE===
At one hundred dollars per month, Deal A needs twenty-four months of projected cash flow to earn back that repair. That is two full years of cash flow.
===SCENE===
At three hundred dollars per month, Deal B needs eight months of projected cash flow to recover the same repair.
===SCENE===
At five hundred dollars per month, Deal C needs just under five months. Same repair. Very different impact.
===SCENE===
Now assume insurance increases by fifty dollars per month. Deal A drops from one hundred dollars to only fifty dollars. Half of its projected margin disappears.
===SCENE===
Deal B falls from three hundred to two hundred fifty dollars per month. That hurts, but there is still meaningful breathing room.
===SCENE===
Deal C falls from five hundred to four hundred fifty dollars. The same fifty-dollar increase is much easier for a stronger-margin property to absorb.
===SCENE===
Now let us say the verified market rent ends up fifty dollars lower than the original projection. Again, every deal loses the same fifty dollars per month, but the weaker deal feels it much more.
===SCENE===
If rent is fifty dollars lower and insurance is fifty dollars higher at the same time, Deal A goes from one hundred dollars to zero. Deal B goes from three hundred to two hundred. Deal C goes from five hundred to four hundred.
===SCENE===
That is the key lesson: the question is not just, is the property cash-flow positive? The better question is, how much room does the property have when your assumptions are wrong?
===SCENE===
The dollar amount is not the only number that matters. You should also compare the cash flow with how much cash you had to invest.
===SCENE===
Suppose each deal required thirty thousand dollars of total cash invested. One hundred dollars per month equals twelve hundred per year, or about a four percent annual cash-on-cash return before taxes and other considerations.
===SCENE===
Three hundred dollars per month equals thirty-six hundred dollars per year, which is about twelve percent on thirty thousand dollars invested.
===SCENE===
Five hundred dollars per month equals six thousand dollars per year, or twenty percent on thirty thousand dollars invested. That looks much stronger, but unusually high returns deserve extra verification because risk or missing expenses may be hiding in the numbers.
===SCENE===
There is no universal minimum cash-flow number. A newer property with low repair risk may justify a different target than an older property with deferred maintenance. A high-cost market may look different from a lower-cost market. Financing and your own reserve position matter too.
===SCENE===
For EM Property Group, I would screen cash flow with five questions: how many dollars remain each month, what is the cash-on-cash return, how much margin survives a stress test, how strong are the reserves, and what property or neighborhood risks are still unknown.
===SCENE===
Deal A, at one hundred dollars per month, gets a weak score. It is technically positive, but it has very little room for error.
===SCENE===
Deal B, at three hundred dollars per month, looks workable under these assumptions. It has enough margin to absorb smaller changes without immediately going negative.
===SCENE===
Deal C, at five hundred dollars per month, looks strongest on cash flow, but we still have to verify why the return is so high and make sure we did not miss expenses, condition problems, or neighborhood risk.
===SCENE===
Instead of chasing one magic number, set a personal minimum that includes a safety buffer. For example, if your goal is at least two hundred fifty dollars per month after realistic expenses and financing, a deal that only projects two hundred fifty gives you no cushion. You may want the initial projection to be higher.
===SCENE===
So is one hundred dollars enough? For a deal with normal repair and expense risk, I would consider that a thin margin. Three hundred gives you more breathing room. Five hundred is stronger if the numbers are real. The higher cash flow is not automatically better, but the stronger buffer usually gives you more ways to survive bad assumptions.
===SCENE===
Five takeaways. One: positive cash flow does not automatically mean a good deal. Two: measure cash flow after realistic expenses and debt. Three: stress-test repairs, rent, and insurance. Four: compare cash flow with cash invested. And five: build a margin above your personal minimum.
