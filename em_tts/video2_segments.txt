A rental property can pass the 1% rule and still be a bad investment. In this video, I’ll show you exactly how that happens using a simple hypothetical deal, and by the end, we’ll decide whether this property is a buy, a negotiate, or a pass.
===SCENE===
The 1% rule is a quick screening tool. It says the monthly rent should be at least one percent of the purchase price. So if a property costs ninety thousand dollars, one percent is nine hundred dollars per month.
===SCENE===
The reason investors like the rule is speed. If you are looking at dozens of properties, it gives you a fast way to eliminate deals that probably will not cash flow. But the word to remember is probably. It is not a full analysis.
===SCENE===
Here is today’s hypothetical example. Purchase price: ninety thousand dollars. Expected monthly rent: nine hundred fifty dollars. Since one percent of ninety thousand is nine hundred, this property passes the 1% rule.
===SCENE===
A beginner may stop right there and think: nine hundred fifty dollars of rent on a ninety thousand dollar property sounds good. But rent is not profit. Now we have to subtract the costs that actually come with owning the property.
===SCENE===
Let’s start with property taxes. We’ll estimate one hundred sixty dollars per month. Taxes vary heavily by location, so this is one of the first numbers you should verify before making an offer.
===SCENE===
Next is insurance. We’ll estimate one hundred dollars per month. Depending on the property, neighborhood, roof, age, claims history, or vacancy, insurance can come in much higher than expected.
===SCENE===
If you use professional property management, let’s reserve ten percent of rent. On nine hundred fifty dollars, that is ninety-five dollars per month.
===SCENE===
For maintenance, let’s reserve seventy-five dollars per month. Some months may be zero. Other months could involve plumbing, appliances, electrical work, or a repair that wipes out several months of cash flow.
===SCENE===
For vacancy, we’ll reserve fifty dollars per month. Even a good rental will eventually have turnover, cleaning, leasing time, or a period when no rent is coming in.
===SCENE===
Then we need a capital-expenditure reserve. We’ll use seventy-five dollars per month for larger items such as a roof, furnace, water heater, windows, or major plumbing work.
===SCENE===
Add those together: one hundred sixty for taxes, one hundred for insurance, ninety-five for management, seventy-five for maintenance, fifty for vacancy, and seventy-five for capital expenses. That totals five hundred fifty-five dollars per month before the mortgage.
===SCENE===
With nine hundred fifty dollars of rent minus five hundred fifty-five dollars of operating expenses, we are left with three hundred ninety-five dollars before debt service.
===SCENE===
Now let’s assume the buyer finances the property. For this example, we’ll estimate principal and interest at about four hundred sixty dollars per month. Your actual payment will depend on down payment, rate, loan type, and financing costs.
===SCENE===
Now the deal changes completely. Three hundred ninety-five dollars before debt, minus a four hundred sixty dollar mortgage payment, gives us negative sixty-five dollars per month. The property passed the 1% rule, but under these assumptions, it loses money.
===SCENE===
This is exactly why the 1% rule should never be your final decision. It does not account for local taxes, insurance, management, repairs, vacancy, financing, or the condition of the property.
===SCENE===
Let’s stress-test it. What if rent is only nine hundred dollars instead of nine hundred fifty? The property still looks close to the 1% rule, but monthly cash flow gets even worse.
===SCENE===
What if insurance comes in fifty dollars higher than expected? That extra fifty dollars goes directly against your margin. Thin deals can turn negative with one bad quote.
===SCENE===
And what if you get a three-thousand-dollar repair during the first year? When a property is already losing money monthly, that repair does not just reduce your return. It increases the amount of cash you have to feed into the deal.
===SCENE===
So what could improve this property? A lower purchase price, higher verified rent, cheaper financing, lower taxes, lower insurance, or a property condition that reduces future repair risk. The goal is not simply to make the 1% rule work. The goal is to create margin.
===SCENE===
Suppose the seller drops the price from ninety thousand to eighty thousand dollars and your financing improves. The same nine hundred fifty dollars of rent now has more room to work. That is why a deal that is weak at one price may become attractive at another.
===SCENE===
Instead of asking only, does it pass the 1% rule, ask five questions. What is the true rent? What are the true operating expenses? What will the financing cost? How much cash flow remains after everything? And how badly does the deal perform when your assumptions are wrong?
===SCENE===
For this ninety-thousand-dollar example, the EM Property Group Deal Scorecard looks like this: rent-to-price ratio, pass. Cash flow, fail. Expense resilience, weak. Financing sensitivity, high. Property condition and neighborhood, still unknown.
===SCENE===
My verdict is PASS at ninety thousand dollars under these assumptions. Not because the 1% rule is useless, but because the full numbers do not leave enough margin. If the price or expenses improve significantly, then I would analyze it again.
===SCENE===
Here are the five takeaways. One: the 1% rule is a screen, not a guarantee. Two: rent is not profit. Three: taxes and insurance can destroy a thin deal. Four: financing matters. And five: always stress-test before you buy.
