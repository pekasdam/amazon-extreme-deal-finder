A rental property can look cheap and still be a bad investment.

And a property that looks expensive can sometimes be the safer deal.

So before you buy your first rental, you need to answer one question:

Does this property actually work after the real expenses are included?

===SCENE===

In this video, I’m going to walk through a hypothetical rental property from start to finish. We’ll look at the rent, the one-percent rule, operating expenses, financing, cash flow, cash-on-cash return, neighborhood risk, and then I’ll stress-test the deal to see what happens when something goes wrong.

At the end, we’ll give the property an EM Property Group Deal Score and decide whether it’s a buy, a pass, or a deal worth negotiating.

This is a hypothetical example for education. The numbers are simplified, and any real property needs its own due diligence.

Let’s start with the property.

Our hypothetical purchase price is one hundred and twenty thousand dollars.

We think the property can rent for fifteen hundred dollars per month.

A beginner might stop right there and say, “That sounds good.”

But those two numbers don’t tell us enough.

===SCENE===

The first thing I want to verify is the rent.

I don’t want the seller’s projected rent.

I don’t want the highest asking rent I can find online.

And I definitely don’t want to use a rent number just because it makes the deal work.

I want realistic comparable rentals.

So imagine we find three similar homes in the same general area.

One rents for fourteen hundred dollars.

One rents for fourteen seventy-five.

And one rents for fifteen fifty.

Same general property type. Similar bedrooms and bathrooms. Similar size and condition.

===SCENE===

For this example, I’m comfortable underwriting the property at fifteen hundred dollars per month.

That doesn’t mean fifteen hundred is guaranteed.

It means it’s a reasonable working number for our hypothetical analysis.

===SCENE===

Now let’s check the one-percent rule.

One percent of one hundred and twenty thousand dollars is twelve hundred dollars.

Our projected rent is fifteen hundred.

So yes, this deal passes the one-percent rule.

===SCENE===

But this is one of the most important things a new investor needs to understand:

Passing the one-percent rule does not mean you should buy the property.

The one-percent rule is a filter.

It tells me, “This might be worth analyzing.”

That’s all.

===SCENE===

Now we move to the expenses.

For this example, let’s estimate property taxes at one hundred eighty dollars per month.

Insurance at one hundred dollars.

Property management at one hundred fifty.

A maintenance reserve of one hundred twenty.

A vacancy reserve of seventy-five.

And another one hundred dollars per month set aside for larger future repairs and capital expenses.

===SCENE===

That gives us seven hundred twenty-five dollars per month in estimated operating costs before the mortgage.

===SCENE===

This is where rental-property math gets dangerous if you oversimplify it.

If somebody tells you:

“The rent is fifteen hundred and my mortgage is six hundred fifty, so I cash-flow eight hundred fifty dollars,”

that is not a real cash-flow calculation.

Taxes exist.

Insurance exists.

Vacancies happen.

Things break.

Property managers get paid.

Roofs, furnaces, plumbing, electrical, appliances, and water heaters eventually cost money.

You may not pay every expense every month, but you still need to account for them.

===SCENE===

Now let’s add financing.

For this hypothetical example, we’ll use approximately six hundred thirty dollars per month for mortgage principal and interest.

Add that to the seven hundred twenty-five dollars of operating expenses.

Now we’re at one thousand three hundred fifty-five dollars per month in total estimated expenses.

===SCENE===

Our rent is fifteen hundred.

Fifteen hundred minus thirteen fifty-five leaves about one hundred forty-five dollars per month.

===SCENE===

That’s only seventeen hundred forty dollars per year.

===SCENE===

So look at what just happened.

At the beginning, the property looked strong.

One hundred twenty thousand purchase price.

Fifteen hundred rent.

It passed the one-percent rule easily.

But once we underwrote the real expenses, our margin got thin.

===SCENE===

Now let’s look at cash-on-cash return.

Suppose the buyer puts thirty thousand dollars down.

After closing costs and initial reserves, let’s say total cash invested is about thirty-five thousand dollars.

Our estimated annual cash flow is seventeen hundred forty dollars.

Divide seventeen forty by thirty-five thousand.

That’s roughly a five-percent annual cash-on-cash return.

A five-percent cash-on-cash return is not automatically good or bad.

It has to be judged against the risk, the neighborhood, the condition of the property, your financing, and your alternatives.

The property could appreciate.

The tenant could pay down part of the loan over time.

Rents could rise.

But I never want those future possibilities to be the only reason the deal works.

I want today’s numbers to give me a margin of safety.

Now let’s do something that a lot of quick deal calculators skip.

Let’s stress-test the property.

===SCENE===

First stress test: what if the rent is fifty dollars lower than expected?

Instead of fifteen hundred, we only get fourteen fifty.

Our estimated monthly cash flow falls from one hundred forty-five dollars to about ninety-five dollars.

That’s not much room.

===SCENE===

Second stress test: what if insurance ends up fifty dollars more per month than our estimate?

Now that ninety-five dollars can fall to around forty-five dollars.

One small change in rent and one small change in insurance have nearly wiped out the monthly cash flow.

===SCENE===

Third stress test: imagine a three-thousand-dollar repair during the first year.

At one hundred forty-five dollars of projected monthly cash flow, it would take more than twenty months of that cash flow just to recover a three-thousand-dollar expense.

That doesn’t mean the property is a terrible deal.

It means the deal has very little cushion at this purchase price.

===SCENE===

And that brings us to the neighborhood.

A spreadsheet can’t tell you everything.

A property making three hundred dollars per month in a high-vacancy area with constant turnover may be less attractive than a property making two hundred twenty-five in a stable neighborhood with consistent rental demand.

===SCENE===

I want to understand vacancy.

Crime.

The physical condition of nearby properties.

Employment.

Schools.

Rental demand.

Property taxes.

Insurance costs.

And whether tenants actually want to stay in that area.

You are not just buying four walls and a roof.

You are buying the income that the location can produce.

===SCENE===

Now I want to score our hypothetical deal.

Here is the EM Property Group Deal Scorecard.

===SCENE===

Rent-to-price ratio: strong. The deal passes the one-percent screen.

Cash flow: weak. One hundred forty-five dollars per month is positive, but the cushion is thin.

Cash-on-cash return: moderate. Around five percent in this example.

Expense resilience: weak. A couple of small changes can erase most of the profit.

Neighborhood: unknown. We cannot score that honestly without researching a real location.

Property condition: unknown. We would need an inspection or a realistic repair assessment.

===SCENE===

So this is not a green-light deal.

It’s a yellow-light deal.

That means I would not rush to buy it at one hundred and twenty thousand dollars.

===SCENE===

Instead, I would ask a better question:

At what price does this deal become safer?

Let’s say we could negotiate the purchase price down enough to reduce our mortgage payment and preserve more cash reserves.

Or maybe we discover that fifteen hundred is actually conservative and the verified market rent is higher.

Or maybe taxes and insurance come in below our estimate.

Any one of those could improve the deal.

===SCENE===

But I do not want to assume those improvements.

I want to verify them.

That’s the difference between hoping a deal works and underwriting a deal.

===SCENE===

So would I buy this hypothetical property?

At one hundred and twenty thousand dollars, with the assumptions we used today, I would not call it an automatic buy.

I would negotiate.

I would verify rent.

I would verify taxes and insurance.

I would inspect the property carefully.

And I would want a bigger margin between the rent and my total monthly costs.

If I can create that margin, the deal becomes much more interesting.

If I can’t, I’m willing to pass.

That mindset matters.

You do not need every property.

You need the right property at the right numbers.

===SCENE===

Before you buy your first rental, remember these five things.

A cheap purchase price does not automatically mean a good deal.

Passing the one-percent rule does not automatically mean a good deal.

Rent is not the same thing as profit.

A deal should survive reasonable changes in your assumptions.

And you should never be afraid to walk away when the margin is too thin.
